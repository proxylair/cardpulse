When Proxy Cards Actually Make Sense (And When They Don't)

📦 Collecting & Community 2026-08-19

Full disclosure up front, since this article touches our own work: CardPulse is run by the same person behind ProxyLair, a custom TCG proxy card design and production studio. We're not pretending to be a neutral third party covering ProxyLair from a distance -- this is our own related business, and we're saying so plainly rather than burying it in fine print. With that out of the way, the actual topic is worth covering on its own merits: proxy cards are one of the more misunderstood parts of the hobby, and the "should I use one" question comes up constantly.

What a proxy actually is. A proxy is a stand-in for a real card -- usually a physical reprint of the artwork and text, sometimes just an index card with the name written on it, used in place of a card you don't own a real copy of. They range from tournament-legal placeholders (a blank card with the name written on it, used temporarily while your real copy is in the mail) to full custom-art reprints meant to look and feel like a premium version of the real thing.

Where they're allowed, and where they're not. This is the part worth being direct about: sanctioned, official tournament play across Magic, Pokemon, One Piece, Lorcana, and Riftbound generally requires genuine cards. Proxies are not a way to play in a store championship or a Regional Qualifier with a card you don't actually own. Where proxies genuinely fit is casual and kitchen-table play -- a lot of Commander playgroups tolerate or even prefer proxies for expensive staples, some local stores allow them for casual formats at their own discretion, and plenty of players use them purely for deck-testing before deciding whether a card is worth buying for real. If you're not sure whether proxies are welcome somewhere, ask the store or tournament organizer directly -- policies vary and change, and this article isn't a substitute for checking.

Where our own price data explains the appeal. This isn't hypothetical -- we've published the actual numbers. In Attack of the Vine!'s chase card breakdown we tracked Belle & Beast - Certain as the Sun at $2,188.63 and Lilo & Stitch - Fun-Loving Friends at $1,681.59. In Riftbound's Vendetta market check Akali, Rogue Assassin was sitting at $2,873.33. Those are real, current market prices for single cards. For a casual playgroup that just wants to see a Belle & Beast or an Akali on the table without spending three figures or more, a well-made proxy is the difference between "never happens" and "happens this weekend."
